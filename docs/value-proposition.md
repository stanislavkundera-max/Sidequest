# Value proposition

## Core copy

**Break the routine. Start living.**

- **Escape autopilot.** Work, gym, scroll, repeat — [App] breaks the loop
  with side quests that pull you out of routine and into something real.
- **Get off the screen, into the world/nature.** Not one more app to
  scroll, but the nudge to put the phone down and actually go live.
- **Never wonder "what should I do?"** Pick from a library of curated side
  quests — small, doable adventures, ready whenever you are.
- **Guided step by step.** No planning, no overthinking — the app walks
  you through each quest so you actually finish it.
- **Keep every moment.** Capture each experience with photos and notes,
  and build a growing collection of memories that prove you lived it.

## If this becomes a habit — what changes for them?

The value prop above sells the single quest. This is the payoff if someone
keeps doing it — what changes about how they feel and who they are, not
just what they did.

- **Interest / aliveness.** Autopilot stops feeling like the default. They
  start noticing things worth trying instead of scrolling past them —
  curiosity becomes a habit, not an occasional mood.
- **Peace.** Regular time outside and away from the screen lowers the
  background hum of stress. Less "I should be doing something productive,"
  more comfort with simply being somewhere.
- **Confidence.** Every finished quest is proof they follow through on
  something, however small. That compounds into "I'm someone who actually
  does things" — not just intent, a track record (the memory collection is
  the receipt).
- **Social relationships.** The social-category quests force real, live
  contact instead of passive scrolling-adjacent contact. Repeated often
  enough, that rebuilds the muscle of reaching out first.

### Backed by research

This isn't just a nice feeling. People who get outside for at least two
hours a week report meaningfully better health and wellbeing than people
who don't — and it doesn't have to be one big trip, short visits add up the
same way (White et al., 2019). A single 90-minute walk in nature measurably
quiets the part of the brain tied to rumination (Bratman et al., 2015). And
on the other side of it: minds running on autopilot are reliably less happy
than minds that are actually somewhere, doing something — regardless of
what that something is (Killingsworth & Gilbert, 2010).

> **Green notes — full citation list (researched 2026-08-21).**
> Six real, checked studies below, split by which claim they back. The
> paragraph above already uses the three strongest (#1, #2, #4) — the rest
> are backup, and all six are raw material for the "science" note planned
> for the Progress tab (`tasks.md` #10). Noted where a study is
> correlational vs. an actual randomized experiment, so nothing overclaims.
>
> **Backs "peace" (nature time → lower stress):**
>
> 1. **White et al., 2019**, *Scientific Reports* — people who spend ≥120
>    min/week in nature report meaningfully better health and wellbeing than
>    those who spend none; holds regardless of age, income, or whether the
>    120 min is one trip or several short ones. Large sample (~20,000
>    people); correlational, not an experiment.
>    [nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-44097-3)
> 2. **Bratman et al., 2015**, *PNAS* — a 90-minute walk in nature (vs. an
>    urban walk) measurably reduced rumination and activity in a brain
>    region tied to depression risk. Small sample (38 people), but an actual
>    randomized experiment — the strongest causal evidence of the six.
>    [pnas.org](https://www.pnas.org/doi/abs/10.1073/pnas.1510459112)
> 3. **Barton & Pretty, 2010**, *Environmental Science & Technology* —
>    meta-analysis of 10 UK studies (1,252 people): "green exercise"
>    reliably improves mood and self-esteem, with the biggest jump from even
>    short/light exposure, then diminishing-but-positive returns after that.
>    Good backing for "doesn't take much," not "needs to be a big trip."
>    [pubs.acs.org](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/es903183r)
>
> **Backs "aliveness / interest" (breaking autopilot, screens):**
>
> 4. **Killingsworth & Gilbert, 2010**, *Science* — the famous "a wandering
>    mind is an unhappy mind" study: people spend ~47% of waking hours
>    thinking about something other than what they're doing, and that
>    mind-wandering (autopilot) predicts unhappiness more strongly than the
>    activity itself does. Directly backs "escape autopilot" as a mechanism,
>    not just a phrase. A later replication adds a genuinely useful nuance
>    for this app's framing: mind-wandering toward something *interesting*
>    lifted mood instead — i.e. autopilot is the problem, curiosity isn't.
>    [science.org](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1192439)
> 5. **Twenge & Campbell, 2018**, *Preventive Medicine Reports* — in a US
>    sample of 40,337 children/teens, higher screen time correlated with
>    lower curiosity, more distractibility, and more trouble finishing
>    tasks. "Less curiosity" is about as direct a hit on "aliveness" as a
>    citation gets. Correlational, and other researchers have publicly
>    contested the causal spin some coverage gave it — cite as
>    "associated with," not "causes."
>    [sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211335518301827)
> 6. **Przybylski & Weinstein, 2017**, *Psychological Science* — the
>    "Goldilocks hypothesis": analyzing ~120,000 UK teens, moderate screen
>    use wasn't harmful, but high daily use (2+ hrs, especially on
>    weekdays) was linked to lower wellbeing. Useful for an honest "it's
>    autopilot/excess, not screens per se" framing, rather than an
>    anti-tech stance the app doesn't actually want to take.
>    [journals.sagepub.com](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797616678438)
